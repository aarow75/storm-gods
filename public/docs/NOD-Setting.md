# The Land of Nod — A Hexcrawl Campaign World

*A Campaign Setting based on John Stater's NOD Magazine, published at landofnod.blog*

---

## The World

The Land of Nod is a sword-and-sorcery world built for exploration. Every major region is a vast hexcrawl — a wilderness mapped in numbered hexes, each one potentially holding a dungeon entrance, a ruined city, a wandering monster lair, a faction stronghold, or nothing at all but empty terrain to cross. The world does not funnel characters toward a story. It presents terrain, powers, and dangers, and lets players decide where to go.

NOD draws its inspirations from real-world ancient civilizations — Phoenician city-states, Roman imperial decline, Asian dynastic court intrigue, African mythology, colonial frontier conflict — and layers classic sword-and-sorcery tropes over them. A bronze-age city-state built on dye monopolies stands next to ruins left by elder things who predate human civilization by geological ages. A declining empire's legions fight nomadic tribes on its crumbling borders while its provincial governors scheme for independence. A plateau of naga-queens arbitrates the eternal conflict between Law and Chaos. The world is not a secondary creation with internal consistency enforced by a single author — it is a GM's toolkit, built issue by issue, region by region, designed to be used in pieces or stitched together into a continuous world map.

**The foundational premise of play**: Characters are not heroes chosen by fate. They are adventurers — mercenaries, wanderers, exiles, scholars, outlaws — who have arrived at the frontier of explored territory. Every hex could be their last. Every city-state has its own interests and will use outsiders as tools. The world does not care whether they survive.

**The Law vs. Chaos axis** runs through the entire NOD world. This is not a moral alignment system — it is a cosmological conflict. Law represents order, civilization, hierarchy, and stasis; Chaos represents dissolution, transformation, primal power, and change. Both have their extremes. Lawful empires become tyrannies. Chaotic wilderness devours everything. The most interesting parts of the NOD world are the contested edges: the places where an old empire is failing and something new is deciding whether to be orderly or wild.

**The planar cosmology** extends beyond the world's surface. Hell is a literal destination, reachable by high-level characters willing to fight their way through Lucifer's domain — a multi-layered underworld divided into distinct circles, each ruled by competing demon lords who war against one another even as they punish the damned. Other planes include analog worlds orbiting the same sun: an ancient, decadent Venus and a war-scarred Mars with their own civilizations.

### The Ages of the World

The NOD world has a layered geological history that its ruins, dungeons, and NPCs reference constantly. Understanding the historical sequence helps GMs explain why the terrain looks the way it does.

**The Pandiluvian Age** — before recorded time, before human civilization, before even the earliest human myths, three elder races dominated the world: the **Elder Things** (alien intelligences whose architecture is inhuman and whose purpose no one living can explain), the **Fish Men** (aquatic civilizations that once held the coastlines and river systems), and the **Lizard Kings** (reptilian empires of the deep tropics and warm seas). Each left ruins. The elder things' buildings still stand in isolated places, utterly unlike any human construction. Fish man temples are found submerged in coastal shallows or swamps. Lizard king ruins are buried under centuries of jungle growth.

**The Rise of Humanity** — at some point lost in myth, human slaves revolted against the Lizard Kings and established the first independent city-states. The mechanisms of this revolt are obscure; the results are not. Humans proliferated. The fish men retreated to deep water. The elder things withdrew — or died. The lizard kings collapsed into isolated, diminished remnants.

**The Nabu Empire** — the first great human empire of the Wyvern Coast region, a sorcerous power that expanded aggressively until a cataclysm of unknown origin destroyed it, leaving behind the City of Death on the River of Death and a desert wasteland called the Crimson Waste where the empire's heartland once stood. The Crimson Waste is now inhabited by the **Warudi**, nomadic desert tribes who maintain oral traditions about the empire they outlasted.

**The Purple Kings** — a dynasty of coastal hill lords who built their wealth on the trade secret of a prized purple dye. They established colonies across the coastline, most of which fell to ruin. Their greatest city, **Ophir**, endured and is the primary civilized hub of the Wyvern Coast today.

**The Nomo Empire** — the world's major historical empire, a late-Roman/Byzantine analog that once held vast territories across multiple regions. Nomo is in the process of failing. Its legions are overstretched. Its provincial governors are asserting independence. Nomadic tribes press its borders. The bureaucracy still functions, after a fashion, and Nomo's law and infrastructure remain in place in areas the empire still nominally controls — but everyone with eyes can see what is coming. The collapse of Nomo is the background condition of half the hexcrawls in the NOD magazine. Every region around it is being shaped by the question of what comes next.

**Hyrcania** — explicitly described as the *first* human empire, predating Nomo. Hyrcanian ruins and lost relics of the Padishah Shahryar appear across the eastern regions, where competing factions race to recover the four imperial relics that would, by ancient law, legitimate a new claimant to Hyrcanian succession.

## Factions

### The City-States of the Wyvern Coast

The Wyvern Coast (NOD 1–3) is the oldest-developed region of the NOD setting, a sun-baked landscape of savannah, desert, and coastal hills built around **Ophir**, the sole surviving city of the Purple Kings dynasty. Ophir is the dominant city-state of the region and the natural base camp for early campaigns — it has merchants, taverns, temples, sages, armorers, and a loose government that tolerates outsiders as long as they spend money and don't cause too much political trouble.

**Ophir's power structure**: Prince Zargo nominally controls the city. The Mercantyl Guild controls commerce. The temples — dedicated to the Phoenician gods Shedu (good), Astarte and Adonis (love and fertility), Kothar-wa-Khasis (artisans), Baal-Zebul (evil), Lotan (the sea), and Melkarth (heroes) — collectively control the moral and cultural life of the population. None of these powers is stable. All of them will hire outsiders when it's convenient.

**Threats to the Wyvern Coast**: Wyverns plague the coastal hills — not intelligent, but dangerous and territorial. Corsairs raid the shipping lanes of the Tepid Sea. Ophidians (serpent-folk) control underground dungeon complexes beneath the hills, occasionally raiding surface settlements. To the north, in the Guelph region, a continuous war against hobgoblin incursions has been grinding on for years. Steppe nomads press from the east.

**The Crimson Waste and its city Rama**: Queen Zabbai rules Rama, an oasis city of 5,250 souls that served as Nomo's capital of the Varudia province. She has declared independence and claims suzerainty over the Crimson Waste — a claim contested by the Warudi nomads who actually live there and by the remnant Nomo legions who technically still own it. Rama has a great marble victory arch and multiple temples; it also has three competing powers (the Queen, the Warudi, and a Nomo loyalist faction) ready to explode at the slightest pressure.

### The Olde Empire of Nomo

Nomo (NOD 31) is the setting's dominant failing power — a civilization built on law, roads, legions, and bureaucracy that has stretched past the point where it can maintain itself. It evokes late-period Rome and the Byzantine successor state simultaneously: senators in the capital plot against one another while frontier provinces declare independence, and the treasury funds legions that can no longer march fast enough to be everywhere at once.

**What Nomo has**: Infrastructure. Roads connect formerly remote settlements. Nomo's aqueducts, legal codes, and administrative traditions have shaped every region the empire ever touched, and those marks don't disappear when the legions leave. A former provincial capital keeps its street grid, its forum, and its Latin-derived language even after seceding. Nomo's cultural legacy outlasts its military power.

**What Nomo is losing**: Authority. The city of Mantu — Nomo's principal port — is "hardening its defenses" and considering independence under the command of Dux Ailio Cynon Gonzorgo, who wants to be its new ruler. Provincial garrisons are underpaid and undersupplied. Nomadic pressure from the desert pushes inward at multiple border points.

**What Nomo will leave behind**: The question that shapes multiple hexcrawl regions. Who fills the vacuum? The city-states of Kisthenes want to summon Tiamat into the material world. The desert nomads want to raid and settle. Local strongmen want to build personal fiefdoms. Nomo's successors will fight over its ruins for generations.

### The Naga Queens of Ende

Ende (NOD 22–24) is a dusty plateau with a distinct flavor — Indian-inspired in aesthetics, structured around four naga-ruled city-states that have each found a different answer to the Law vs. Chaos question. The Nagas are not simply monsters; they are queens of ancient and legitimate civilizations with complex courts, trade relationships, and philosophical positions. The conflict on the plateau is ideological as much as military: which version of cosmic order should govern?

**The four city-states** represent different theological stances — some are aligned with Law, some with Chaos, some are contested, and the plateau's geography means they cannot avoid one another. Merchant caravans, diplomatic missions, and mercenary troops cross between the city-states constantly.

**Player position in Ende**: Characters arrive as outsiders in a civilizational dispute older than their own culture. Each naga queen has uses for competent, deniable adventurers. Each city-state has internal factions that don't fully agree with their queen's position. The hexcrawl terrain between the city-states is ancient, ruined, and dangerous.

### The Demon Lords of Hell

Hell (NOD 11–15) is not a distant metaphysical concept — it is a destination. Getting there requires fighting through appropriate dungeon layers; getting out requires either defeating Lucifer personally or finding one of the hidden exits that other, luckier travelers have discovered. Hell is designed for high-level characters who have exhausted most of what the surface world can offer.

Hell is divided into distinct circles with their own terrain, populations, and rulers. The circle of **Gehenna** — punishment for greed — is a vast wasteland of metallic sand with rivers of molten metal and basalt mesas. Its nominal ruler is **Mammon**, Grand Prince of Avarice, who governs through subterfuge and subversion rather than direct command. But Mammon's control is contested by four demon lords who resist his authority: **Amon**, **Maphistal**, **Paymon**, and **Pazuzu**. These factions war constantly against each other and occasionally against Mammon's agents.

**Gehenna's races**: Seven distinct peoples inhabit this circle — the Arkusites (hairless gnolls serving demon lords), the Harrites (pterodactyl-like kobolds who torture captives in cobalt towers), the Lamuresti (copper-skinned elves devoted to Mammon), the Sarrimites (shape-shifting goblins tied to the demoness Tyrana), the Uccenites (ophidian nomads who animate mummified dead), the Xshayathiyans (serpent-folk who rule grand cities of precious metals), and the Xulites (bronze-skinned human slavers). Political alliances between these groups shift constantly depending on which demon lord is ascendant.

**Neutral parties**: Mulciber, Hell's smith, maintains political neutrality and will deal with any faction — or any mortal visitor — who has something worth his time.

### The Factions of Mu-Pan

Mu-Pan (NOD 8–10) is the NOD setting's Asian-inspired region — a landscape of geothermal valleys, icy mountain peaks, and dense jungle organized around city-states with complex, unusual political structures. The city of **Dinsan** (population 6,000) is built atop a 200-foot plateau and illustrates Mu-Pan's governing principle: power is rotational, decided by which faction's founder-statue faces the sun.

**Dinsan's six factions** each hold absolute power when the sun illuminates their patron statue through a network of bronze mirrors:
- **Sohei** — military monks in red lacquered armor; austere, loyal, effective
- **Samurai** — knights who follow a strict code of honor; can be reasoned with within that code
- **Shugenja** — ascetic priests who impose moral prohibitions; inflexible but predictable
- **Ninja** — organized as a criminal syndicate; cooperative when paid, dangerous when crossed
- **Wushen** — monkey-folk priests who emphasize celebration and irreverence; genuinely friendly but chaotic allies
- **Descendants of the Builders** — the faction of enslaved laborers who built Dinsan and who, when empowered, govern with the specific fury of the long-oppressed

**Play in Mu-Pan**: Characters who arrive in Dinsan need to quickly understand which faction currently controls power and calibrate their behavior accordingly. The faction in power changes daily. Requesting an audience with the city's Sohei warrior-monks at the wrong time of day means dealing with the Ninja syndicate instead.

**The Jade Empress**: The overarching power of Mu-Pan, ruling from somewhere in the region's mountains or jungles, old and powerful and not easily contacted. Her relationship to the individual city-states is tribute, not governance.

### The Virgin Woode's Colonial Contest

The Virgin Woode (NOD 19–21) is the setting's colonial frontier — a narrow coastal plain of oak, hawthorn, and birch forest backed by the Purple Mountains on a continent called Antilia. The impetus for colonization was competition between the declining Nomo Empire's successor city-states: Guelph established Port Janus and the silver colony of Argentum; Antigoon established the trading post of Dweomer Bay in response.

**The problem with colonization**: The Virgin Woode already has inhabitants. **Wild elves** and **cunning fey** resist human expansion actively and don't distinguish between competing human factions — to them, all the colonists are the same threat. **Treants** and woodland creatures add natural hazard to political danger. Out at sea, **Atlantis** has resurfaced and pursues its own agenda that predates any of the colonial powers' claims.

**The pirate element**: Tremayne's filibusters — a pirate operation that emerged from the colonial contest itself — raid shipping from both colonial powers, profiting from the instability they helped create.

**Tone**: The Virgin Woode is the NOD setting's most politically complex region. There is no clean good-vs-evil framing: the colonists are exploitative but often individually sympathetic, the fey resistance is justified but often brutal, the pirates are criminal but operate by a clear code, and Atlantis is alien enough that no one really knows what it wants. Characters can work for any faction or none.

### The Trollheims and the Northern Wilds

The Trollheims and the broader region of Og (NOD 26–29) are the setting's northern wilderness — cold, mountainous, populated by trolls, ogres, giants, and worse. Civilization here means something different: **King Mogg** rules a city-state of giant-kind, with its own brutal politics, succession crises, and the grudging respect of every surrounding people who have tried and failed to conquer it.

**Og's peculiarities**: The Og region features dwarves and gnomes alongside giants — the classic small-folk in close proximity to the classic large folk, both contesting the same mountain terrain. Shadow horses haunt certain passes. Enlightenment temples appear in unexpected places. The northern wilderness is never simply empty; it is full of things that have been there longer than any kingdom.

## Locations

### Ophir — The Gate of the Wyvern Coast

Ophir is the Wyvern Coast's only surviving great city — heir to the Purple Kings, center of Phoenician-inspired culture, and the obvious starting point for any campaign in NOD 1–3. It has everything a party needs: temples that sell healing, sages who sell information, armories, mercenary brokers at the Bloody Bones tavern, and a court wizard named Jamala who may be employed as a patron.

**What characters can do here**: Accept employment from Prince Zargo's court, the Mercantyl Guild, individual merchant patrons (Riyad the Salt Merchant, Ubata the Caravan Master, Karma the Gentlewoman), or private clients with less official purposes. Fight in the pit at Mulla's for prize money and reputation. Hire retainers. Buy information from Lathba the sage. Have Nabe' the alchemist identify a strange potion.

**What they shouldn't ignore**: The secret societies operating in Ophir's shadows. The corsairs who raid shipping from the Tepid Sea. The ophidians in the tunnels beneath the hills who have been probing the city's foundations. The war to the north in Guelph that drains Ophir's military capacity.

### The City of Death

The ancient capital of the Nabu Empire, standing on the River of Death in the middle of the Crimson Waste. The empire that built it collapsed centuries ago; the city is now a ruin of a scale that suggests the Nabu were far more powerful than anything currently existing. The City of Death is the NOD setting's ur-dungeon: a vast, partially collapsed metropolis full of traps, monsters, and Nabu-era artifacts that no current civilization fully understands.

**GM notes**: The City of Death is intentionally difficult to reach (crossing the Crimson Waste) and dangerous to explore. It is the kind of location that generates rumors in Ophir's taverns for years before characters actually go there. The Warudi nomads know its outer geography but will not enter the buildings.

### Pnath — The Passage to Hell

The specific location of the descent into Hell is kept appropriately vague — the NOD setting does not provide a single dungeon map from surface to Hell, because different entries to the underworld exist in different regions. What the setting establishes clearly is that Hell is *reachable*: it requires fighting through appropriate dungeon levels, which requires characters of sufficient experience and equipment to survive the journey.

**The practical implication**: Hell functions as a high-level campaign destination that GMs can attach to any dungeon complex of sufficient depth in any region. Once characters have hexcrawled through the Wyvern Coast, explored Mu-Pan, and cleared most of what Nomo has to offer, Hell is where they go next.

### The Plateau of Kisthenes

Kisthenes (NOD 32) is the setting's most immediately dangerous region: ancient city-states controlled by rival sorcerer-kings who are actively, knowingly trying to summon Tiamat — the primordial chaos-dragon — into the material plane. This is not an abstract goal. They have a plan. They are working on it. Characters who learn what is happening and choose to stop it are taking on a campaign-level objective with civilization-scale stakes.

**The city-states of Kisthenes** do not cooperate on the Tiamat project — they compete. Each sorcerer-king wants to be the one who controls the summoning, not merely a contributor. This internal competition is the one structural weakness characters can exploit.

### Rama and the Crimson Waste

Rama is the most immediately playable location in the Nomo region: a real city with a functioning population and a political crisis in progress. Queen Zabbai has declared independence. The Warudi tribal elders dispute her claim to their desert. A Nomo loyalist faction in the city thinks the Queen has made a catastrophic mistake. A Nomo army column may or may not be on its way to reassert control.

**Adventure hooks that write themselves**: The Queen needs information about Warudi movements that she can't get through official channels. The Nomo loyalists need allies who won't be recognized as imperial agents. The Warudi elders want someone to recover a sacred artifact stolen by a Ramite merchant. Every faction has a job. Only one faction can win.

### Dinsan and the Mirror-Clock

Dinsan in Mu-Pan is the setting's most mechanically interesting city-state: power rotates daily based on which bronze mirror illuminates which ancestor-statue. Running Dinsan as a GM means tracking which faction currently holds power and playing their response to outsiders accordingly.

**Practical tip**: The Sohei will grant audiences formally and demand formal behavior. The Shugenja will refuse any audience that violates current moral prohibitions — which change. The Ninja will offer to help but are almost certainly extracting information in exchange. The Wushen will invite characters to a banquet first and discuss business later, sometimes much later. The Builders' Descendants will be interested in what outsiders know about organizing labor uprisings in other regions.

## Campaign Arcs

### Arc 1 — The Wyvern Coast: Founding an Adventuring Company

**Starting point**: Ophir. Characters arrive with minimal resources and need to establish themselves.

**Early hooks**: Clear the ophidian tunnels under the merchant district. Escort a caravan through wyvern territory to Guelph. Recover a stolen religious relic for a temple that can't pay in coin but offers healing in perpetuity. Investigate the corsair fleet's new harbor somewhere on the Tepid Sea.

**Mid-campaign**: The political situation in Ophir escalates — Prince Zargo's court is compromised by a secret society, the Mercantyl Guild wants to fund an expedition to the City of Death, and the Guelph war is going badly. Characters now have enough reputation to be courted by factions, not just hired by individual patrons.

**Late campaign**: The City of Death. Either the Guild expedition or the characters' own ambition drives them into the ancient ruins. What the Nabu left behind is not simply treasure — it includes knowledge of what destroyed their empire and why the River of Death runs where it runs.

### Arc 2 — The Fall of Nomo: Picking a Successor

**Starting point**: The Crimson Waste, approaching Rama.

**Context**: Nomo is visibly failing. Characters arrive in a region where every settlement is recalculating its loyalties. They're outsiders — potentially valuable to whoever hires them, potentially threatening to established local powers.

**Choices**: Support Queen Zabbai's independence movement. Work for Nomo loyalists trying to hold the empire together. Side with the Warudi nomads and help them formalize their territorial claims while the empire is weak. Play kingmaker in Mantu's succession crisis. All four choices generate enemies and potential allies in the other three factions.

**Endgame**: Whoever controls Rama, Mantu, and the roads between them effectively controls the old Varudia province. If Kisthenes' sorcerer-kings succeed in summoning Tiamat while the region is politically fragmented, no one has the military force to respond.

### Arc 3 — The Ende Question: Law or Chaos?

**Starting point**: Arrival on the plateau of Ende via the Klarkash Mountains.

**Premise**: The four naga-ruled city-states have been in a cold war for decades, each representing a different position on the Law-Chaos axis. Characters are outsiders with skills that every queen wants to deploy. The question is not "defeat the villain" — the question is "what kind of world do you want to live in?"

**The complication**: Chaos is not simply evil. The Chaotic city-states are more creative, more tolerant of outsiders, and more willing to bend rules in the characters' favor. The Lawful city-states are more stable, more predictable, and more reliably safe. Both sides have their monsters and their sages. The ending the characters reach depends on which city-states they strengthen and which they weaken.

### Arc 4 — Into Hell

**Starting point**: Any deep dungeon complex with a downward path that hasn't ended.

**The pitch**: Characters at high level have achieved most of what the surface world offers. They have heard rumors of Hell for years — the soul-markets, the demon lords' competing courts, Mulciber's workshop where legendary weapons are forged. Descending is a one-way commitment unless they fight their way back out.

**Hell as a campaign environment**: The demon lords are not a unified enemy. Mammon's agents in Gehenna do not coordinate with the powers of the other circles. Mortal visitors who demonstrate competence become valuable — as proxies, as mercenaries, as intelligence assets. The question in Hell is not "survive" (though that is also a question) — it is "what are you actually here for?" Most characters who descend want something specific: the return of a dead companion, a weapon that only Hell can produce, information about a binding whose source is in the planar records.

**Getting out**: Going through Lucifer is the canonical exit. There are other options, but they require information that must be found within Hell itself.

## Creatures of NOD

### Wyverns

The apex predators of the Wyvern Coast hills are not particularly intelligent but are territorial, venomous, and fast on the ground as well as in the air. They nest in cliff faces and descend on livestock and travelers with equal enthusiasm. The coast is named for them for a reason — there are a lot of them.

**In play**: A wyvern encounter is a serious but manageable threat for a group of moderate experience. The primary danger is the venomous tail sting, which causes rapid incapacitation and death if untreated. Wyverns do not negotiate and cannot be bribed. They can sometimes be distracted by sufficiently large prey.

### Ophidians

The serpent-folk of the underground — ancient, intelligent, deeply hostile to surface humans. Ophidians were present before the Purple Kings, possibly before humanity established itself in the region at all. Their underground cities are extensive and well-defended. Surface raids are rare but purposeful: they want specific things (slaves for labor, specific magical ingredients, territorial buffer zones) rather than simply killing.

**Roleplaying ophidians**: They are not monsters who exist to be fought. They are a civilization that predates Ophir and will outlast it. An ophidian diplomat is a possible contact in any scenario that involves the tunnels beneath the Wyvern Coast. They are also genuinely dangerous — ophidian soldiers are disciplined and fight in coordinated units.

### Trolls and Giants of Og

The northern wilderness is populated by trolls (regenerating, implacable, deeply territorial), ogres (brutal but organized enough to employ as mercenaries by other factions), and giants of several varieties. King Mogg's city-state is entirely giant-staffed. A character with enough resources and political standing can potentially negotiate passage through Og rather than fighting — the giants have wants, primarily tribute and respect.

**The shadow horses**: Specific Og hexes contain shadow horses — creatures that appear equine until they are very close. Their nature is ambiguous in the setting notes; GMs can interpret them as fey, as creatures of the Ethereal Plane, or as something stranger unique to the region.

### The Races of Gehenna

See the Factions section above for the seven distinct peoples of Gehenna. All of them serve as potential contacts, employers, or enemies in Hell. None of them are reliably trustworthy, but most of them have consistent interests that can be predicted and played against.

### Warudi Nomads

Not monsters — the desert tribes of the Crimson Waste are human, organized, and have survived where empires have not. They navigate the desert by knowledge accumulated over generations and trade in information about routes, water sources, and the movements of other groups. Warudi elders are among the best sources of historical information about the Nabu Empire available outside the City of Death itself.

**In play**: Warudi encountered in the Crimson Waste are not automatically hostile. They are suspicious of strangers and deeply protective of their territorial claims. Characters who demonstrate respect for Warudi customs and do not press for information they haven't earned can establish a working relationship that proves invaluable for navigating the Nomo-region campaign arc.

## Running the Land of Nod

### It Is a Hexcrawl, Not a Plot

The NOD setting does not have a main story. There is no chosen one, no central villain whose defeat resolves everything, no prophecy the characters must fulfill. It has regions, each of which contains dozens of adventure seeds, several interacting factions, and terrain to cross. The job of the GM is to present the terrain and populate the rumors. The job of the players is to decide where to go.

This requires a specific GM mindset: **the map is not a set of encounters waiting to happen in order**. Most hexes will never be visited. The characters will choose a direction and follow it, and the GM needs to be ready to run whatever is in that direction. Prep should prioritize the areas near current character position, not the "important" locations — the important locations are wherever the characters decide to go.

### City-States as Social Dungeons

Every major settlement in NOD — Ophir, Rama, Dinsan, Mantu — has a political structure that functions like a dungeon in social terms. There are layers (public commerce, guild politics, court intrigue, criminal networks), locked areas (private audiences, inner temple rites, secret society meetings), and resources that can be unlocked with the right key (reputation, money, alliance, information). Characters who invest in city relationships get access to things that purely wilderness-focused characters don't.

The temples are particularly important. In Ophir, different temples offer different services (healing, curse removal, information about the divine), follow different gods with different priorities, and have their own political relationships with the secular powers. Which temple the characters patronize communicates something about them to the rest of the city.

### Factions Want Things

Every major faction in NOD has stated goals and a reason those goals are currently frustrated. GMs should keep a simple reference card:

| Faction | Wants | Is Blocked By |
|---|---|---|
| Queen Zabbai (Rama) | Legitimate independent sovereignty | Warudi opposition, Nomo loyalists, lack of military strength |
| Warudi Elders | Recognized territorial rights in the Crimson Waste | Rama's claim, Nomo's legal system, internal tribal divisions |
| Nomo Loyalists | Imperial restoration | The empire's actual collapse, Queen Zabbai, frontier military failure |
| Sorcerer-Kings of Kisthenes | Summoning Tiamat | Each other's competition, mortal interference, the binding seals |
| Mammon | Total control of Gehenna | Four rival demon lords, mortals who won't stay out of Hell |
| The Jade Empress (Mu-Pan) | Unknown | Unknown |

The Jade Empress's wants being listed as "unknown" is a feature, not a bug. Her agenda drives a late-campaign reveal in Mu-Pan.

### The Hexcrawl Rhythm

Standard NOD play uses a simple daily structure:
- **Morning**: Check for getting lost (if in open terrain without landmarks)
- **Travel**: Number of hexes crossed depends on terrain type
- **Encounter check**: 1-in-6 chance per travel period (reduced with a ranger in the party)
- **Rest**: Camp, watches, and the night encounter check

Tracking resources (food, water, torches, arrows, spell components) is not optional in this style of play. Characters who don't manage logistics will die of exposure or starvation as often as they die in combat. This is intentional — the wilderness is a hazard, not just a travel animation between interesting locations.

### Incorporating NOD Into Other Systems

The NOD magazine is written primarily for Blood & Treasure (Stater's own retroclone) and compatible old-school systems. For RuneQuest, Dragonbane, or similar BRP/OSR systems, the following conversions are straightforward:

- **Hit Dice** translate directly to a Brawn or CON proxy for monster toughness
- **Alignment (Law/Chaos/Neutral)** maps cleanly to RuneQuest's cosmic alignment concepts
- **Saves** can be replaced with appropriate characteristic rolls
- **Monster abilities** are descriptive enough to port without mechanical conversion in most cases

The setting material — factions, locations, history, encounter seeds — requires no conversion at all. It is geography and politics, not game mechanics.
